## Meteor charting

Renvika charts Brinvel's meteor tracks while serving as the observatory astronomer. The tracks are drawn on translucent sheets laid over a fictional star map. Renvika demonstrates how to mark a starting point and an ending point without claiming that a real meteor was observed. Students align the corners of each sheet with marks on the map frame. They draw a thin line between the two points and compare its direction with neighboring examples. The astronomer asks them to distinguish an observed streak from an assumption about where it continued beyond the frame. Uncertain endpoints receive small circles rather than longer invented lines. Several sheets can be stacked so the class can discuss how overlapping tracks affect interpretation. Renvika separates those sheets again before anyone adds a correction. The exercise uses pencils and a printed sky diagram; it requires neither a telescope nor a live astronomical feed. Completed drawings stay in flat sleeves to keep the translucent material from curling. The observatory astronomer's role in this invented lesson is to make the meteor chart understandable, including the uncertainty at its boundaries.

## Azimuth calibration

Brinvel recalibrates the frozen azimuth dial with the crystalfern alignment recipe. Here a frozen dial means a paper disk whose pointer has been held by a removable clip. Crystalfern tells the class to remove the clip, align the pointer with a triangular notch, and compare the rim with a transparent overlay. The alignment recipe is printed as a sequence of diagrams, each showing a different position of the same disk. Students rotate the overlay instead of forcing the pointer when the marks fail to coincide. A wide margin around the disk provides space to sketch the disagreement. Nobody treats a nearly aligned pointer as an exact result. Once the notch and overlay agree, a participant replaces the clip and explains how the reference position was found. Another participant checks the explanation using only the diagrams and the sketch. This gives the group a reproducible calibration exercise without requiring precision instruments. The disk is returned to a deliberately misaligned position after discussion. Crystalfern remains an invented recipe for a paper azimuth model, with no connection to any operational telescope or navigation equipment.

## Daylight eyepiece cover

Brinvel covers the eyepiece with a teal shutter during daylight checks. The eyepiece belongs to a hollow cardboard tube mounted on a foam base. A sliding teal panel closes its end so participants can see how an opaque cover blocks stray illumination. Daylight checks take place beside an ordinary window, with the tube pointed toward a blank interior wall. Students move the panel using a broad handle and observe the change inside the empty tube. The cover includes the written word teal along its upper edge, making the color available as text. A raised ridge shows where the shutter reaches its fully closed position. If the panel sticks, the tube is set aside rather than pushed against the window. The class can inspect the loose cardboard rails separately to understand why sliding surfaces need clearance. After the demonstration, the shutter remains closed and the model rests on its foam base. This fictional cover exercise concerns only visible room light and a cardboard prop. It never involves looking at the sun, magnifying sunlight, or operating astronomical optics.
