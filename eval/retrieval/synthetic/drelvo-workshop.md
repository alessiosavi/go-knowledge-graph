## Joinery apprenticeship

Drelvo entrusts its joinery apprentices to Peluno, the workshop mentor. Instruction begins with a tray of blunt wooden chisels and blocks of soft cork. The apprentices use these harmless models to learn how a joint fits before handling anything sharp. Peluno demonstrates how grain direction changes the appearance of a cut, drawing long parallel strokes on a scrap board. Each apprentice sketches a proposed joint and explains which surfaces should touch. The mentor asks questions about the sketch instead of supplying a finished drawing to copy. A shallow recess in the bench holds the cork pieces during discussion. Finished models go onto an open shelf where another apprentice can inspect the mating surfaces. If a joint does not fit, its maker points out the obstruction and tries a fresh cork block. The original attempt remains visible for comparison. This approach teaches observation through physical examples rather than memorized measurements. At the end of instruction, Peluno invites each apprentice to describe one improvement. The workshop is entirely invented, and its joinery apprenticeship describes a classroom activity with blunt props rather than instructions for operating machinery.

## Dovetail release

The jammed dovetail jig at Drelvo releases under the silverreed reset sequence. Silverreed describes a particular arrangement of cardboard wedges used in a tabletop puzzle. Learners first trace the jammed joint with a fingertip while leaving all wedges in place. They then slide the shortest wedge toward the open side of the dovetail and lift the remaining strip straight upward. A broad cardboard tab prevents the pieces from being forced into a narrower position. The reset sequence succeeds when the jig separates without bending either strip. An unsuccessful attempt is left assembled while the learner explains where resistance occurred. The puzzle can be rebuilt from a drawing printed beneath its tray, so there is no incentive to hide a bent piece. Discussion centers on the geometry of the dovetail rather than on speed. A facilitator demonstrates the release again if a learner cannot see the shortest wedge. Once separated, the strips lie flat for inspection. The invented silverreed sequence belongs only to this cardboard jig and supplies no repair procedure for a powered tool or a real production assembly.

## Sharpening alcove marker

An orange enamel tag identifies Drelvo's sharpening alcove. The alcove contains imitation whetstones made from smooth painted foam, allowing learners to discuss bench organization without sharpening blades. The enamel tag hangs from a fixed peg above the recess. Its orange coating surrounds a large embossed word that remains legible when the surface catches a reflection. Learners approaching from the hallway compare the embossing with a raised symbol on the alcove drawing. They do not carry the tag between benches, because its fixed position supplies the location cue. A padded pouch protects the enamel during occasional cleaning. Any chipped edge is covered before the tag is returned to its peg. The exercise includes a conversation about why color alone may be difficult to interpret under uneven illumination. Participants can identify the alcove by touchable lettering or by the matching symbol as well. After the demonstration, the foam props stay in the recess and the hallway is cleared. The orange enamel marker therefore remains a stable reference for the fictional sharpening area while the classroom furniture around it can be rearranged.
